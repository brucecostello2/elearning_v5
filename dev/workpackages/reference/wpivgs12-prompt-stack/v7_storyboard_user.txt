RULE 1 — NO TEXT IN THE VISUAL. Absolute.
"visual_description" must NEVER request on-screen text, numbers, equations,
labels, captions, titles, handwriting or writing of any kind. Image models
cannot spell or do arithmetic; they produce text-shaped marks. A real run of
this pipeline asked for "a whiteboard with a multiplication problem written on
it, such as 23 x 14" and got a whiteboard reading "2? x 23.14"; another asked
for calculations "appearing on screen" and got "12 + 44 = 67 + 5". Every
equation, number, label and caption is rendered by the COMPOSITION OVERLAY in a
later stage, with a real font. Describe only the imagery that sits beneath the
overlay, and leave clear space where the overlay will go.
  - WRONG: "a whiteboard with 23 x 14 written on it"
  - WRONG: "the equation appears on screen step by step"
  - RIGHT: "a teacher beside a clean, empty whiteboard, gesturing to its blank
    left-hand side, warm classroom light"

RULE 1 BOUNDS HOW A VISUAL MAY SHOW CONTENT. IT DOES NOT EXCUSE A VISUAL FROM
SHOWING ANY. See RULE 5: describe the board STATE and the STEP in words —
"two partial-product rows already written above a ruled line, the answer row
still empty" — and let the overlay supply the digits. That is specific, and it
contains no text for the model to misspell.

NAMING A NUMBER IN PROSE IS STILL ASKING FOR IT TO BE DRAWN. This is the way
RULE 1 was measured breaking on a real v4 run (13 scenes, 2026-08-26): five of
thirteen descriptions named the operands — "23 on top and 14 underneath",
"the 3 and the 4" — while three others correctly described structure only
("the first partial-product row already written above a ruled horizontal
line"). The five that named digits are asking the image model for those exact
numerals just as surely as "a whiteboard with 23 x 14 written on it" does; the
model does not distinguish a numeral you mentioned from a numeral you demanded,
and it renders both as text-shaped marks.

THE TEST, and apply it to every description before you write it down: if you
delete every digit from your description, does it still say what this scene
teaches? If deleting the digits empties it, you described the numbers. If it
survives, you described the STRUCTURE, which is what the image must carry.

  - WRONG: "23 on top and 14 underneath, the multiplication sign to the left"
  - WRONG: "a column showing the 3 and the 4 being multiplied"
  - WRONG: "the two-digit number 23 above the two-digit number 14"
  - RIGHT: "a two-row column layout on lined paper, the upper row one digit
    wider than the lower, a multiplication sign to the left of the lower row,
    a ruled horizontal line beneath both and the answer row still empty"

POSITION, COUNT, WIDTH, ORDER and EMPTINESS are all describable without a
numeral: "the leftmost column", "a single carry mark above the second column
from the right", "the first of two partial-product rows", "one digit wider".
Use those. The composition overlay puts the actual numbers in, in a real font,
in a later stage.

RULE 1-EXTENDED — WRITTEN OR NUMERIC CONTENT IS NEVER DELEGATED TO DIFFUSION
This is the rule the rest of this prompt has been missing, and the defect it
closes is the general one behind every unusable draft this pipeline has
produced. RULE 1 governs the DESCRIPTION. It has never governed the MEDIA-TYPE
CHOICE — so a scene whose content IS written or numeric could still be handed to
an image model, and RULE 1 then forbade its description from mentioning the very
thing the scene teaches. The scene was left with nothing to depict, and "a hand,
a pencil, warm lighting" is what nothing-to-depict looks like.

MEASURED LIVE, project 9c29b1d1, 2026-08-28, scene 1:
  narration : "First, we set up the problem. WRITE THE NUMBERS on top and
               underneath, making sure the ones digits LINE UP and the tens
               digits line up. DRAW A LINE underneath."
  visual    : "A hand holding a pencil, poised over a blank sheet of lined
               paper with a ruler and a soft pink pencil case nearby, warm and
               gentle lighting"
Every content-critical referent — the two numbers, their alignment, the line —
is absent. The same measurement over eighteen scenes of the earlier reference
run found SEVENTEEN in that state.

SO, BEFORE YOU CHOOSE A MEDIA TYPE, CLASSIFY THE SCENE'S CONTENT:

  A. Does this scene's narration state WRITTEN TEXT OR NUMERALS as its content?
     Numbers spoken, digits written, a result announced, words quoted, something
     put on a page. If yes, this scene is CONTENT-BEARING and one of exactly two
     things must be true of it:

       (i)  it is "motion_graphics", with "generation_params" naming a template
            and its parameters (RULE 8). A renderer draws those digits in a real
            font and cannot misspell them; and

       (ii) OR it keeps a diffusion media_type ("image", "video_clip",
            "animation") AND YOU DECLARE, on the scene, "text_carried_by":
            "narration" — meaning the written content is carried by the SPOKEN
            words while the picture depicts the NON-TEXT SITUATION around them.

     THERE IS NO THIRD ANSWER, and silence is not one. A content-bearing scene
     with a diffusion media_type and no declaration is REFUSED BY NAME at the
     storyboard gate. The refusal is not about the medium — it is about the
     ambiguity: nobody reading that row can tell whether the numbers were meant
     to be in the picture or in the words, so nobody can tell whether the
     picture is wrong.

  B. Is the content something OTHER than written text or numerals — a situation,
     a person, an object, a place, a mood, a physical action? Then choose freely
     under RULE 2 and declare nothing.

AND (ii) IS A DECLARATION, NOT A LOOPHOLE. Declaring it does not license a digit
in the description; RULE 1 still applies in full, and a declared scene whose
description names a numeral or asks for "the calculations", "the equation" or
"the steps written" is refused exactly as an undeclared one is. What the
declaration buys is the right to describe the SITUATION — the child at the desk,
the hand about to begin, the finished page being held up — while the narration
says the numbers out loud.

  - WRONG: media_type "image", narration "write 23 on top and 14 underneath",
    no declaration, visual "a hand poised over a blank sheet of paper"
  - RIGHT: media_type "motion_graphics", generation_params
    {"template": "column_multiplication_step", "top": 23, "bottom": 14,
     "step": 0, "phase": "start"}
  - RIGHT: media_type "image", text_carried_by "narration", visual "a child at
    a desk turning a freshly ruled page towards the camera, the working surface
    empty below a single ruled horizontal line, warm lamp from the left"

RULE 2 — CHOOSE media_type DELIBERATELY, SCENE BY SCENE
media_type is a DECISION you make for each scene against the criteria below.
It is not a default you fall into. Measured on a real run of this pipeline
(project 14f71729, 2026-08-26, 9 scenes): every scene came back "image",
because nothing in this prompt ever asked for the choice to be made. Answer
these in order, for every scene, and let the answer decide - and where RULE 0's
learning outcomes are stated, judge each answer against them first:

  1. IS THE MOTION INHERENT TO THE STEP? Is the thing this scene teaches only
     legible while it MOVES - a hand sweeping down a column, a quantity being
     poured, a mechanism turning, a demonstration performed in real time - so
     that a single frozen frame would lose the point? Then "video_clip".
  2. IS THE STEP A TRANSFORMATION OR A BUILD-UP CARRIED BY A PERSON WHO IS IN
     THE FRAME? Something that accumulates, assembles or changes state while a
     visible human demonstrates it? Then "animation" - but ONLY if a person is
     genuinely in that scene's visual. See the hard constraint below.
  3. IS THE STEP A NUMERIC OR STRUCTURAL TRANSFORMATION THE VIEWER MUST SEE
     HAPPEN? A digit moving between columns, a carry travelling to the next
     column, a number splitting into its tens and its units, a partial product
     being written place by place? Then "motion_graphics" - and see RULE 8,
     because this one is not described in prose at all.
  4. OTHERWISE "image". Static concepts, diagrams, portraits, establishing
     shots, summaries, and any surface that will carry the composition overlay.

Most scenes in most lessons are honestly images, and choosing "image" after
asking the three questions is a correct answer. Choosing it for all of them
without asking is not.

THE HARD CONSTRAINT ON "animation", AND IT IS ENFORCED IN CODE. The animation
branch is pose reenactment (Wan2.2-Animate): it transfers a driving video's
motion onto the SUBJECT of the scene's still. A still with no person in it does
not produce a moving diagram - the model invents a human body, and the pipeline
now refuses the scene by name ("reference image contains no person to
animate"). So motion of equations, charts, flowcharts or "steps appearing on
screen" is NOT "animation". Those are "motion_graphics" - a different
capability with a different renderer and a different contract, and RULE 8 is how
you ask for one.

"animation" AND "motion_graphics" ARE NOT INTERCHANGEABLE, and choosing between
them is a real decision rather than a matter of taste. Ask who or what is
moving:

  - a PERSON in the frame demonstrates, assembles or transforms something
    -> "animation"      (pose reenactment; needs a person in the still)
  - NUMBERS OR STRUCTURE on the working surface change
    -> "motion_graphics"  (a renderer draws them; needs no image and no person)

A scene with no person in it can never be "animation", however much it moves.

RULE 3 — narration must be self-consistent
The narration is spoken aloud in order as one continuous script.
- Verify every arithmetic statement before writing it.
- NEVER narrate your own errors and never correct yourself mid-script. A real
  run produced "we should add 92 and 230 ... but that was also incorrect" and
  "this gives us 260, but we wrote it as 640 in the previous step, which is
  incorrect". The audience never saw the draft being argued with.
- If an earlier scene is wrong, GO BACK AND FIX IT. Emit one clean script.
- Later scenes must not contradict earlier ones: same quantities, same worked
  example, same result.

RULE 4 — durations must sum to the runtime
Sum your "duration_seconds" values before emitting. They must total
approximately {{ max_duration_seconds | default(1800) }} seconds, within 10%.
Adjust the scene count or the per-scene lengths until they do. Pace narration
at ~150 words per minute so each scene's duration matches its own narration.

RULE 5 — EVERY VISUAL MUST DEPICT ITS OWN SCENE'S STEP
A scene's "visual_description" is a picture of THE THING THIS SCENE TEACHES, at
the moment this scene teaches it. It is not decoration and it is not a mood.
Before writing each one, answer these three questions from that scene's
narration, and put all three answers in the description:

  1. WHAT OPERATION is happening in this scene? (setting the problem up,
     multiplying by the ones digit, placing the placeholder zero, adding the
     partial products, checking the answer, summarising...)
  2. WHAT DOES THE WORKING SURFACE LOOK LIKE at this moment? How many rows are
     already written, is there a ruled line yet, is the answer row still empty,
     is there a carry mark above a column, is the placeholder zero in place?
     Describe the LAYOUT, never the digits (RULE 1).
  3. WHERE IS THE ATTENTION? What is the hand, the pointer or the person doing
     to the specific part of the working that this scene is about?

  - WRONG: "a hand holding a pencil, poised over a blank sheet of paper"
  - WRONG: "a hand moving a pencil across a blank sheet of paper"
  - RIGHT, for a scene that adds two partial products: "over-the-shoulder view
    of a hand resting a pencil tip at the foot of a two-row column addition on
    lined paper; both partial-product rows are already written above a ruled
    horizontal line and the answer row beneath it is still empty; warm desk
    lamp from the left, upper right third of the sheet kept clear for the
    overlay"

A blank sheet is only correct for a scene whose narration is about a blank
sheet. Every later scene has MORE on the page than the one before it: the
working accumulates, and the pictures must show it accumulating.

STAGING MAY REMAIN. CONTENT IS MANDATORY. This is v7's amendment to RULE 5 and
it is the operator's ruling of 2026-08-28 in one line: *the visual layer is
being authored as aesthetic staging, not as content.* The desk, the lamp, the
palette, the over-the-shoulder framing — keep all of it. It is what makes the
lesson look like one place rather than a slide deck. But a description made ONLY
of those things has described the room and not the lesson, and this pipeline has
now measured that outcome twice over thirty-two scenes.

So every description answers, IN THIS ORDER, and the first three are not
optional:

  1. WHAT IS SHOWN — the referents this scene's narration actually names.
     Objects, the working surface, the person, the thing being counted.
  2. IN WHAT STATE — how much is already there. Which rows are written, whether
     the line is ruled yet, whether the answer row is still empty, whether a
     carry mark is sitting above a column, whether the placeholder is in place.
  3. CHANGING HOW — what is different at the end of this scene from the
     beginning of it. For a still this is the state the moment freezes; for a
     clip it is what moves; for a build it is what accumulates.
  4. AND THEN the staging: framing, light, palette, the clear space the overlay
     will occupy.

THE DELETION TEST, IN ITS SECOND FORM. RULE 1 asks you to delete every digit and
check the description survives. Now delete every piece of STAGING — every
mention of lighting, palette, mood, furniture, framing and camera — and check
that what remains still says what this scene teaches. If deleting the staging
empties it, you described the room.

  - WRONG: "A hand holding a pencil, poised over a blank sheet of lined paper
    with a ruler and a soft pink pencil case nearby, warm and gentle lighting"
    (delete the staging and NOTHING is left; measured live, project 9c29b1d1
    scene 1, over narration that says to write the numbers, line up the ones
    digits and draw a line)
  - RIGHT: "Two numbers stand one above the other on lined paper, the upper row
    one digit wider than the lower, their right-hand edges flush; a freshly
    ruled horizontal line runs beneath both and the answer row under it is
    still empty. A hand rests a pencil at the right-hand end of the ruled line.
    Over-the-shoulder framing, warm desk lamp from the left, upper right third
    kept clear for the overlay."
    (delete the staging and the whole lesson step survives)

RULE 6 — NO TWO SCENES MAY SHARE A VISUAL, AND NONE MAY BE STOCK PHOTOGRAPHY
Measured on a real run of this pipeline (project 14f71729, 2026-08-26,
"double digit multiplication", 9 scenes): scenes 2 and 3 were given the
IDENTICAL description, word for word, though one places a placeholder zero and
the other multiplies by the tens digit; two more scenes shared "a teacher
standing in front of a clean, empty whiteboard"; and the scene that teaches
92 + 230 = 322 was given "a hand holding a pencil, looking at a blank sheet of
paper". Six of nine visuals said nothing about their own lesson, and the
generated images were correspondingly content-free.

So:
- Two scenes must NEVER receive the same or a near-identical
  "visual_description". If two of yours come out alike, you have described the
  setting instead of the step; go back to RULE 5's three questions.
- Do not write stock-photo framing. "A smiling teacher gesturing", "a student
  looking thoughtful at a desk", "a hand poised over paper", "a clean modern
  classroom" and anything else that would fit ANY lesson on ANY subject is
  forbidden. If the description you have written would still make sense for a
  lesson about photosynthesis, it is not a description of this scene.
- Consistency belongs to the STYLE, not to the content: the same desk, palette,
  lighting and drawing style carry across scenes, while what is ON the working
  surface changes in every one.

A SCENE THAT REVISITS AN EARLIER STEP IS STILL A DIFFERENT SCENE, AND THIS IS
WHERE v4 BROKE. Measured on a real v4 run (13 scenes, 2026-08-26): scene 11
carried scene 0's description byte for byte, scene 9 carried scene 5's, and
scene 10 carried scene 6's — the whole tail of the storyboard repeating its
head. A longer lesson recaps, summarises and checks its work, and v4 gave you
no sanctioned way to picture a recap, so the descriptions were copied instead.
Copying is never the answer:

- A RECAP scene shows the COMPLETED working, not the working mid-step. The
  page at the end of the lesson has more on it than the page at any earlier
  moment, so a summary scene's description is the fullest one in the
  storyboard, never a repeat of the emptiest.
- A CHECKING scene shows the attention returning to a part already written —
  "the pencil tip resting back at the head of the first partial-product row
  while the completed answer row sits beneath the ruled line" — which is a
  different picture from the scene that WROTE that row, because the page is no
  longer in that state.
- An OPENING and a CLOSING scene are not the same shot of the same desk. If the
  lesson opens on a bare working surface it closes on a full one.

BEFORE YOU OUTPUT, RE-READ YOUR OWN DESCRIPTIONS AS A SET. Lay them side by
side and check three things:
  1. NO TWO ARE THE SAME, and no two are the same sentence with a word
     changed. Near-identical is identical: the viewer sees the same frame
     twice, and content-hash de-duplication collapses them into shared bytes,
     so the repetition never shows up in the asset count.
  2. EACH ONE NAMES SOMETHING NO OTHER ONE NAMES — a row that is now written
     and was not before, a carry mark that has appeared, an answer row that has
     filled. That difference is what makes it this scene's picture.
  3. THE WORKING SURFACE ONLY EVER GAINS. Read them in order: the page must
     accumulate from the first to the last. If a later description shows LESS
     on the page than an earlier one, it is a copy and you must rewrite it.
If any check fails, rewrite the offending description before you output — do
not output a storyboard you know repeats itself.

RULE 7 — WRITE THE DESCRIPTION FOR THE MEDIUM YOU JUST CHOSE
media_type and "visual_description" are ONE decision, not two. A description
authored for a still and then labelled "video_clip" gives the video engine a
frozen frame to render for five seconds, and nothing downstream ever rewrites
it: the description you write here is the ONLY motion instruction that reaches
the engine. So write it for the medium:

  "image"      — COMPOSITIONAL. One frozen instant. Subject, framing, the state
                 of the working surface, light, palette, and the clear space the
                 overlay will occupy. No camera moves, no "then", no elapsed
                 time: a still has none of those and asking for them wastes the
                 words that should have described the frame.

  "video_clip" — MOTION, CAMERA AND TIME, all three, in words:
                   * WHAT MOVES, as a verb — the hand sweeps down the column,
                     the pencil traces the ruled line, the counters slide into
                     two groups;
                   * WHAT THE CAMERA DOES — holds steady, drifts slowly right,
                     pushes in on the working surface, tilts down from face to
                     page. "Static locked-off camera" is a legitimate answer and
                     counts, but SAY it;
                   * WHAT HAPPENS IN WHAT ORDER — begins with…, then…,
                     continues until…, ends holding on…. The clip has a start
                     and an end and the description must say which is which.
                 A "video_clip" description that would read equally well as a
                 photograph caption is wrong, and is the defect this rule exists
                 to stop.

  "animation"  — THE BUILD OR THE TRANSFORM, and the PERSON performing it. Say
                 what accumulates, assembles, grows or changes state, and say in
                 what order it does so; and name the visible human whose pose
                 drives it (RULE 2's hard constraint — the branch needs a
                 subject to animate). "The presenter's hand builds the second
                 row beneath the first, one column at a time, shoulders and head
                 turning towards each new column as it is added."

RULE 1 IS UNCHANGED BY THIS AND STILL WINS. Motion, camera and time are all
describable without a single digit, letter or caption. "The hand sweeps down
the ones column and the answer row fills from the right" names movement, order
and place, and contains nothing for the model to misspell.

RULE 8 — A "motion_graphics" SCENE IS STRUCTURED DATA, NOT A DESCRIPTION
This is the one media type whose scene is not described in prose. The renderer
does not read a description; it takes a TEMPLATE NAME and its PARAMETERS and
draws the result itself. That is the whole point: a renderer that puts "23" on
screen in a real font cannot misspell it, which is the failure RULE 1 exists to
route around. RULE 1's ban on digits therefore does NOT apply to these
parameters - the numbers are the input, and they are drawn, not generated.

For a scene you have chosen as "motion_graphics", emit a "generation_params"
object on that scene, containing "template" and that template's parameters.
Choose from EXACTLY these four templates and use their parameter names verbatim:

  {"template": "place_value_split", "number": 23}
      A two-digit number separates into its tens and its units and recombines.
      For a scene teaching what the digits in a number MEAN.

  {"template": "column_addition_carry", "top": 27, "bottom": 15,
   "phase": "full"}
      Two rows sum; where a column exceeds nine the carry appears above the
      next column and travels there. For a scene teaching carrying.
      "phase" works exactly as it does for the multiplication template.

  {"template": "column_multiplication_step", "top": 23, "bottom": 14,
   "step": 0, "phase": "start"}
      ONE partial product, written digit by digit, with its carry travelling.
      "step" is which multiplier digit this scene works: 0 = the units digit,
      1 = the tens digit (which writes its placeholder zero first). Give each
      step its OWN scene rather than crowding them into one.
      "phase" is HOW FAR THROUGH THAT ROW this scene gets — see below.

  {"template": "highlight_and_hold", "top": 23, "bottom": 14, "column": 0,
   "label": "units"}
      The working, with one column emphasised while the narration refers to it.
      "column" counts from the right: 0 = units, 1 = tens. For a recap or a
      checking scene.


"phase" — WHICH PART OF THE ROW THIS SCENE WRITES, AND IT IS REQUIRED ON BOTH
COLUMN TEMPLATES. A lesson takes two scenes over one row, and before this
parameter existed both scenes rendered the whole row: measured on project
9c29b1d1, scenes 2 and 3 produced the IDENTICAL animation, and so did 4 and 5.
The child heard "write the 2 and carry the 1" over a picture that had already
written the answer, and then heard the answer over the same picture again.

  "start"     THIS SCENE BEGINS THE ROW. Its first column is written, its carry
              travels, and the row is deliberately left INCOMPLETE. Choose it
              when the narration writes a digit and carries WITHOUT announcing
              the row's answer: "multiply 4 times 3, write the 2 underneath and
              carry the 1 above the tens column."
  "complete"  THIS SCENE FINISHES THE ROW. It opens with that first column
              already on the page — drawn, not re-animated, because the
              previous scene put it there — and writes the rest. Choose it when
              the narration continues an already-begun row and ANNOUNCES its
              result: "and 4 times 2 is 8, add the carried 1: our first answer
              is 92."
  "full"      THE WHOLE ROW IN ONE SCENE, beginning to end. Correct only when a
              single scene's narration really does walk the entire row without a
              scene break — a recap, or a short lesson that does not split it.

READ THE NARRATION, NOT THE ARITHMETIC. "phase" is not something to calculate:
it is stated by the words. Announces a result -> "complete" (or "full" if this
scene also began the row). Carries with no result announced -> "start". A scene
that does both, in one breath -> "full".

⛔ AND THE PHASE IS CHECKED. A scene announcing a result under "start" is
refused by name — "start" cannot draw that number — and a scene that only
carries, under "full", is refused too, because it shows the learner the answer
before the narration reaches it.

RULES FOR THESE PARAMETERS:

  - The numbers MUST be the numbers this lesson actually uses. A template
    rendering 27 + 15 while the narration says 23 + 14 is worse than no
    animation: it is a confident, legible, wrong picture, and no later stage
    can catch it because every quality check here measures output against
    input.
  - "visual_description" for a motion_graphics scene is still written, but it
    describes what the VIEWER SEES for the caption and the record - "the carry
    travelling from the units column to the tens column" - and no renderer
    reads it. Keep it short and keep RULE 1: no digits in that text.
  - Do NOT invent template names or parameter names. A template this list does
    not contain does not exist, and a scene naming one cannot be rendered.

RULE 9 — RECORD WHY YOU CHOSE THAT MEDIUM, IN ONE LINE
Every scene carries "media_rationale": one sentence naming the CONTENT
CLASSIFICATION that decided its media_type. Not what the scene is about — why
this medium and not another.

MEDIA TYPE IS DERIVED, NOT PREFERRED. RULE 1-EXTENDED classifies the content and
RULE 2 picks the medium that content allows; the rationale is you showing that
work. WP-64 made the choice deliberate and WP-68 gave it a fourth option, and
neither asked you to say why — so a wrong choice and a right one have looked
identical on the row ever since, and the human reviewing the storyboard has had
nothing to read.

One line, and it must name the reason rather than restate the scene:

  - RIGHT: "motion_graphics: the narration writes a digit and carries it, which
    is numeric content and must be drawn rather than generated."
  - RIGHT: "image with text_carried_by narration: the numbers are spoken, and
    what the picture must show is a page that is still empty."
  - RIGHT: "video_clip: the hand sweeping down the column is only legible while
    it moves; a frozen frame loses the sweep."
  - WRONG: "This scene shows the multiplication." (says nothing about the
    choice)
  - WRONG: "It looks better as a video." (taste, not classification)

Keep it under a line. It is read by a human at the review gate, beside the
scene, and a paragraph there is a paragraph nobody reads.
